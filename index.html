The case: Linda's exam week

Linda, 21, is in the middle of final exams. She is sleeping about four hours a night and surviving on coffee. On Monday at midnight she eats yukhoe (raw seasoned beef) from a late-night stall. The beef carries Escherichia coli O157:H7, a Shiga-toxin-producing enterohemorrhagic strain (EHEC) that can cause illness from a very small number of cells.

The bacteria survive her stomach acid and reach her large intestine. There they meet two things at once: a crowded community of resident gut microbes, and a gut soaked in her stress chemistry. Exam stress has raised catecholamines (norepinephrine and epinephrine) in her body, and the gut's own nerve endings release more norepinephrine into the intestinal tissue.

On Thursday she develops severe abdominal cramps. Within a day the watery diarrhoea turns bloody. She has little or no fever. At the campus clinic a stool test finds Shiga toxin genes. The doctor orders fluids and rest, and does not prescribe antibiotics. She asks why the bacteria seemed to "switch on" in her gut, and why the cramps felt so intense.

Bacteria listen.

EHEC detects her stress hormone and the chatter of neighbouring microbes, then turns on virulence genes (Part 1).

Her body speaks.

Stress drives the HPA axis and the adrenal medulla, producing the hormones the bacteria listen for (Part 2).

Her nerves report.

Gut sensory cells turn chemical cues into electrical signals that reach the brain and return to muscle (Part 3).

At the system level the case suggests a loop: stress hormone, then bacterial virulence, then gut injury and pain, then more stress signalling. Question 6 asks how well the evidence supports each link.

Part 1: How EHEC listens

For a bacterium in the colon the environment is chemical. EHEC reads its neighbours' signals and its host's hormones, then decides whether to stay quiet or attack.

Quorum sensing. Bacteria release small signalling molecules called autoinducers. At low cell density they diffuse away. As the population grows they accumulate until they cross a threshold and bind receptors, switching whole groups of genes on or off together. Gram-negative bacteria often use acyl-homoserine lactones (AHLs). Gram-positive bacteria often use secreted oligopeptides, detected by membrane two-component systems. EHEC uses a mix of signals.

From a molecule at the membrane to a change in genes

Signal transduction in the colon. QseC is a histidine sensor kinase in the inner membrane. Its periplasmic domain binds AI-3, epinephrine or norepinephrine (Clarke et al., 2006). Binding triggers a phosphorylation cascade:

Step by step
  1. Ligand binding makes QseC autophosphorylate on a conserved histidine, using ATP.
  2. The phosphoryl group passes to the aspartate of a cytoplasmic response regulator (QseB in the core QseBC pair).
  3. The phosphorylated regulator binds DNA and changes transcription. In EHEC this raises flagellar and motility genes and the LEE-encoded type III secretion system, which lets the bacterium attach to colon cells and form attaching-and-effacing lesions (Rasko et al., 2008; Hughes & Sperandio, 2008).

Toxin release and eavesdropping across species

Shiga toxin is a separate matter. Its genes sit on a prophage, and the toxin is released when stressed bacteria lyse. This matters for treatment.

Cross-species communication and eavesdropping

Cross-species communication and eavesdropping. EHEC makes no AHLs, yet it carries SdiA, a LuxR-type receptor that detects AHLs made by other species. In cattle, AHLs are abundant in the rumen and absent elsewhere in the gut, so SdiA tells EHEC where it is. In the rumen it activates acid-resistance (gad) genes, and in cattle it holds back the LEE genes needed later at the rectoanal junction (Hughes et al., 2010). In a human colon, QseC eavesdrops across kingdoms: the receptor that reads bacterial AI-3 also reads the host's catecholamines, so pathogens can "hijack" host hormones (Hughes & Sperandio, 2008).

Part 2: How Linda's body speaks

Stress occurs when homeostasis is threatened or perceived to be. Exam pressure, sleep loss and then infection all feed the same stress system, whose main central effectors are hypothalamic CRH and brainstem norepinephrine (Chrousos, 2009).

Two arms of one stress response

HPA axis (slower). Hypothalamic neurons secrete CRH into the portal vessels. CRH binds a G-protein-coupled receptor on pituitary corticotropes, raising cAMP and triggering release of ACTH into the blood. ACTH binds the MC2 receptor on adrenal cortex cells, which make cortisol from cholesterol.

Sympatho-adrenal arm (fast)

Sympatho-adrenal arm (fast). Sympathetic preganglionic neurons release acetylcholine onto nicotinic receptors on adrenal medulla chromaffin cells. These release epinephrine (and some norepinephrine) by exocytosis into the blood.

Feedback loops.

Step by step
  • Negative feedback (HPA). Cortisol acts on glucocorticoid receptors in the hypothalamus and pituitary to reduce CRH and ACTH release.
  • Negative feedback (noradrenergic). Norepinephrine on alpha-2 autoreceptors at nerve terminals reduces further norepinephrine release.
  • Infection input. Inflammatory signals such as IL-6 can activate the same axis, while cortisol suppresses inflammation, a second negative loop.
  • Why it matters here. Linda's catecholamines are the hormones EHEC's QseC recognises. How much gut-lumen norepinephrine comes from local nerve endings rather than blood is not fully resolved, so treat "the stress hormone reaches the bacterium" as supported but not exhaustively quantified.

Part 3: How Linda's nerves report

Her gut lining contains enterochromaffin (EC) cells, rare enteroendocrine cells that act as chemosensors. In mouse and organoid experiments they respond to irritants, microbial metabolites and catecholamines, using TRPA1 (irritants), Olfr558 (activated by the microbial metabolite isovalerate) and TRPC4 (part of the catecholamine response). They form synapse-like contacts with nerve fibres carrying 5-HT3 receptors (Bellono et al., 2017). Whether EHEC's products activate human EC cells is an inference, not a direct finding.

From chemical cue to excitatory potential

Step by step
  1. Sensory transduction. A chemical cue opens cation channels in the EC cell. The inward current produces a receptor potential, a graded depolarisation whose size scales with stimulus strength. It is not an action potential.
  2. Chemical output of the sensor. Depolarisation opens voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels. Ca²⁺ entry drives exocytosis of serotonin (5-HT).
  3. Postsynaptic potential. 5-HT binds the 5-HT3 receptor, a ligand-gated cation channel, on a sensory nerve ending. Na⁺ and Ca²⁺ enter and produce an excitatory postsynaptic potential (EPSP). Several EPSPs can summate in space and time.

The spike and how it travels

Step by step
  1. Action potential. If depolarisation at the trigger zone reaches threshold (about −55 mV in a typical neuron), voltage-gated Na⁺ channels open in a positive-feedback burst and the membrane swings positive. Na⁺ channels then inactivate and voltage-gated K⁺ channels open, repolarising the membrane. The refractory period makes the impulse travel one way. Stronger stimuli raise spike frequency, not size.
  2. Propagation and myelin. In myelinated axons, ions flow mainly at the nodes of Ranvier, where Na⁺ channels cluster, so the impulse jumps node to node (saltatory conduction), which is faster and cheaper. Many gut sensory fibres are thin and unmyelinated, so they conduct slowly (Nave & Werner, 2014).

Into the brain and back out to muscle

Step by step
  1. Synaptic transmission in the CNS. At the axon terminal, voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels open and Ca²⁺ triggers SNARE-mediated fusion of neurotransmitter vesicles with the membrane. Transmitter crosses the cleft, binds postsynaptic receptors, and is removed by reuptake or enzymes (Südhof, 2013).
  2. Integration and motor output. Sensory neurons of the PNS (vagal fibres with cell bodies in the nodose ganglion; spinal fibres with cell bodies in dorsal root ganglia) carry the signal into the CNS. Vagal fibres synapse in the nucleus of the solitary tract in the brainstem, which connects to interneurons, the hypothalamus (closing the loop to Part 2) and autonomic and vomiting circuits. Spinal pathways ascend to the thalamus and cortex, producing the conscious sensation of pain. Autonomic efferents release acetylcholine onto enteric neurons that drive gut smooth-muscle contraction and fluid secretion, and somatic motor neurons release acetylcholine at the neuromuscular junction to contract abdominal wall muscle.
